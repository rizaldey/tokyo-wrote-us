Chapter One

Two Tickets to Tokyo

February 11. Tokyo.

CHAPTER ONE

February 11. Tokyo.

The city was cold in that romantic kind of way. The kind that makes your breath visible and your life suddenly feel like an indie film soundtrack should be playing somewhere in the background. Minus the rain. My self-proclaimed best friend was arriving from Manila. First solo international trip. First time in Japan. First time navigating a country where even the vending machines looked intimidating. And the funny part? I was the only person he knew in Tokyo. For ten years, I had built a life here. Learned the train lines. Learned how to survive summers, winters, heartbreaks, and Japanese tax forms. I became the friend abroad. The reliable one. The Tokyo friend. Lol. Don't ask me about kanji, though. I still know almost nothing about them. Somehow, I still passed N3. Yes, pat me on the back. So naturally, everyone assumed: "He must be so excited to see you." And I mean...Yes. But also? There was Theo. Theo, the guy I only knew through stories and late-night conversations. A flight attendant from one of those famous airlines. Honestly, I can't even remember which one. He was supposed to arrive from Canada. Kris talked about him so highly. And by highly, I mean the kind of highly where the man apparently had no flaws. Which, honestly, in 2026? Feels suspicious. Nobody is that perfect. We were supposed to meet at Otsuka Station, my old workplace for a few years. The whole time, I kept wondering whether Kris would survive the commute from Narita Airport to Otsuka by himself. Look, I'm sorry I couldn't pick him up. I gave him everything he needed to survive the journey. Google Maps. Train screenshots. Emergency instructions. Emotional support messages. Best friend duties failed. I know. I told myself I'd make up for it by touring him around the city because February 11 was the only day he actually had plans with me. Yes. Only the arrival day. Long story short, he managed. When I arrived at Otsuka, I saw him dragging his suitcase down the stairs. The stairs. How could this become even more comical? I mean, hello, Kris? Elevators exist. This is Tokyo. One of the biggest cities in the world. But no judgment. I was just happy to see my friend. Because before the romance, before the Tokyo love story unfolded, before Theo... There was me. The one familiar thing in a city of millions. The translator. The emergency contact. The unpaid tour guide. The emotional support local. "Puta ka," he said. "Good to see you." Don't blame us. Colonizers be colonizing. "Welcome to Tokyo," I laughed. "How was the flight?" "Hmmm. I haven't slept much... but it was fun." We hadn't even talked properly yet when he suddenly broke the news. "Theo isn't going to Tokyo anymore." And just like that, the entire energy shifted. The guy he was supposed to meet for the very first time in Tokyo wasn't coming anymore. My best friend, who had never traveled alone before, had just landed in one of the biggest cities in the world carrying equal amounts of excitement, fear, and emotional delusion... For nothing. Trying to look calm. Trying to look cool. Meanwhile, I could practically hear his internal monologue screaming: What if I get lost? Why did I even take the risk of coming here? Did I just fuck up? What if I die on the Yamanote Line? And honestly? Valid concerns. Because while everyone else thought this was a love story about two people finally meeting... I knew the hidden plotline. A boy leaving home for the first time. Trusting a city he had never seen before. Trusting feelings he didn't fully understand yet. Trusting that somewhere between Narita Airport and a Tokyo train station, something meaningful was waiting for him. And somehow, I was standing right at the beginning of it. Then came the inevitable thought. The meet-up. Tokyo Station, probably. Or Shibuya. Somewhere painfully movie-like. Neon lights. Cold air. People rushing past while time slowed down for exactly two idiots making eye contact for the first time. I imagined Theo standing there, scanning the crowd nervously. And Kris, exhausted from the flight, emotionally unstable, carrying way too many expectations inside one suitcase, suddenly spotting him. That kind of smile. The dangerous kind. The kind that makes people book flights and believe in timing again. Maybe they would've laughed awkwardly at first. Maybe they would've stood there for a second, not knowing whether to hug or wave like strangers pretending not to feel too much. And beside them? Me. Holding everyone's emotional baggage in addition to the actual luggage. Watching my best friend forget every survival instinct he'd learned from traveling alone the second Theo smiled at him. Cinema. Absolute cinema. Except none of it actually happened. Because Theo never made it to Tokyo. And maybe that's why I keep imagining the scene so clearly. Because sometimes the stories that stay with us the longest are the ones that almost happened. As the single friend who had somehow become emotionally employed full-time, I took my responsibilities very seriously. If Kris flew all the way to Japan carrying a suitcase full of feelings and unrealized romantic tension, then it was now my civic duty to make sure he still had the time of his life. Because technically, he wasn't ditched. Life just... happened. Theo couldn't make it to Japan anymore. No villains. No cheating scandal. No dramatic airport chase scene. Just bad timing and complicated realities. Which, honestly, feels more painful sometimes. Still, I refused to let the trip become sad. Not on my watch. So I did what every responsible best friend in every chick flick eventually does: I became the tour guide. First stop: Senso-ji Temple. Because if your almost-love story is going through a minor emotional collapse, the least you can do is ask the gods for guidance. The city was freezing that afternoon, but Asakusa already felt alive. Warm lights glowing from little shops. Steam rising from food stalls. Tourists taking blurry photos with numb fingers. Meanwhile, the two of us stood in front of the omikuji booth like our emotional futures were about to be decided by folded paper. "Okay," I told him seriously. "We need daikichi energy today only." "What happens if we get bad luck?" he asked. "We ignore it and continue being delusional." "Ah." He nodded. "So your usual lifestyle." "Puta ka." We both shook the metal fortune containers dramatically. Mine came first. Bad luck. Again. At this point, I'm convinced Senso-ji personally has an issue with me. Kris laughed so hard he almost folded in half. "BRO. EVERY TIME?" "Every single time," I said, staring at the paper like it had ruined my career. "What does it say?" I scanned it dramatically. "Basically: stay humble, ugly." He nearly choked laughing. Then it was his turn. He unfolded his paper carefully while I translated the Japanese for him. Lucky. Of course. And underneath it, one line quietly sat there like the universe itself wanted a cameo in this story: Love will take time. We both went silent for a second. "Hmmm." I looked at him suspiciously. "Are the gods listening?" He smiled softly. The kind of smile people make when they're trying not to hope too much. Afterward, we walked toward the Sumida River cruise. The city suddenly felt slower there. Softer somehow. Cold wind brushing against our coats. The river reflecting gray winter light. Tokyo Skytree standing quietly in the distance while boats passed like scenes transitioning in a romance movie nobody was emotionally prepared for. And somewhere during the walk, the conversation drifted back to Theo. Not in a heartbreaking way. More in a what-could-have-been kind of way. Like imagining him there beside us. Walking with Kris under the Tokyo sky. Talking about random things. Sharing awkward silences. Taking photos neither of them would ever post because somehow the best moments always stay private. February was still cold, but a few cherry blossom trees had already started blooming early. Tiny pink petals against winter air. We stopped under one of them for a moment. And I think that's when I saw it happen. That small return of hope inside him. Because maybe love doesn't always arrive exactly when you planned it to. Maybe sometimes it misses the flight. Maybe sometimes timing fails. Maybe sometimes the person is real, but the moment just isn't ready yet. But standing there beneath those early blossoms, I think Kris still believed that someday, in this universe or another one, he'd get his chance. A real date under cherry blossoms. Not imagined. Not postponed. Not almost. And me? I just stood there beside my best friend, freezing in the Tokyo wind, pretending I wasn't emotionally invested in a love story that technically hadn't even started yet. We eventually boarded the Sumida River cruise, mostly because walking around Tokyo in February starts feeling like a paid internship for hypothermia. Inside the boat, everything suddenly felt calmer. Warm lights. Heated seats. Couples quietly taking photos by the windows like they were filming a skincare commercial. Tokyo Skytree glowing dramatically in the distance while the river carried us through the city like we were transitioning into the second act of a romance movie. And somehow, somewhere between Asakusa and emotional instability, Kris decided it was time to interview me about my love life. Dangerous topic. "So..." he said while staring outside. "What's going on with your love life?" I laughed immediately. Not a cute laugh either. The exhausted kind. The kind divorced aunts make at family reunions. "What love life?" Because honestly? My romantic history had been drier than airplane chicken. It had been four years since I properly dated someone. Maybe longer. At this point, my love life wasn't even inactive. It had entered archaeological territory. Scientists could excavate it. And somewhere along the way, I got weirdly comfortable with it. I became the single friend. The emotionally available but romantically unavailable one. The "I'm focusing on myself right now" final boss. Meanwhile, everybody around me kept falling in love like it was buy-one-take-one season. "I don't know," I admitted dramatically while staring out at the river like a woman in a K-drama. "I think I stopped believing in love." Kris looked at me for exactly two seconds before replying: "Or maybe nobody has loved you properly yet." Excuse me??? Who gave this man dialogue? I looked at him in complete silence while Tokyo floated behind him like he was delivering the emotional climax of the movie. Then he added: "Maybe you deserve to be loved this time. Try opening up a little." And suddenly, the cruise got quieter. Or maybe my brain just muted everything after that sentence. Because the scary thing about being single for a long time isn't actually being alone. It's becoming too good at it. You start romanticizing independence. You convince yourself peace is better than vulnerability. You build routines so stable that the idea of someone disrupting them feels illegal. And honestly? After years of failed timings, almost-relationships, emotionally unavailable people, and whatever the hell 2022 was... I stopped expecting love to happen to me. Not in a sad way. More like a retired athlete. Like, Thank you, everyone. I had a good run. Or not. So I just stood there quietly while the boat passed under another bridge, asking myself something deeply inconvenient: Am I actually ready to meet someone new? Maybe. I mean, it's not like I was helping destiny out. I wasn't on dating apps. No soft-launch situationships. No "wyd" messages at midnight. No emotionally confusing gym crushes. If love was going to find me, it would have to arrive organically. Like in the movies. A random encounter. A missed train. A coffee shop moment. Two people accidentally reaching for the same convenience store sandwich. Something effortless. Something that didn't feel forced. And maybe that sounds delusional in a city like Tokyo, where millions of people pass each other every single day without making eye contact. But as the cruise carried us down the Sumida River beneath the early cherry blossoms, a tiny part of me wondered: What if destiny was already somewhere in this city... Running slightly late like everybody else in Tokyo? By the time we reached the end of the cruise, the sun was already beginning to disappear behind the skyline. Tokyo Tower glowed in the distance, standing watch over the city as evening settled in. "Let's head back to Shinjuku," I told him. Dinner sounded good, but if I was being honest, I wanted him to see Kabukicho. Not because of the restaurants. Not because of the nightlife. But because every first-time visitor deserves to experience that moment when Tokyo finally becomes Tokyo. The train ride back was quiet. Outside the window, the city slowly transformed. Office buildings gave way to illuminated billboards. Streets grew brighter. Crowds thicker. And then we emerged from Shinjuku Station. Kabukicho was already alive. Neon signs stretched toward the sky. Giant screens flickered overhead. Music spilled out from open doorways. Thousands of strangers moved through the streets beneath a sea of electric color. For a moment, Kris simply stood there. Looking up. Taking it all in. The city reflected in his eyes. I lifted my camera. Click. A splash of red light crossed his face. For someone who had spent most of his life under tropical skies, Tokyo's winter suited him surprisingly well. He stood there in an oversized trench coat layered over an all-denim outfit, looking like he had accidentally wandered out of an independent film. Click. Blue and gold danced across the fabric as the neon signs flickered overhead. His features had always been striking in a quiet way. A sharp nose. Slightly tanned skin. Dark eyes that seemed to notice everything even when he pretended not to. The kind of face that became more interesting the longer you looked at it. Click. A smile appeared. The genuine kind. The kind that arrives when someone forgets they're being watched. And suddenly, he looked younger. Lighter. Like the weight he'd been carrying since Theo canceled had loosened its grip for a moment. I kept photographing him while the city roared around us. Because somehow, standing beneath those neon lights, he looked exactly where he was supposed to be. And yet... I couldn't stop thinking about the version of this night that never happened. The one where Theo was here. It was a red day on the calendar. The kind of day that quietly gives people permission to stay out longer than they should. So we did. One bar became two. Two became three. And somehow, we eventually found ourselves in Shinjuku Ni-chome. Now, if you've never been there, imagine narrow alleyways glowing beneath neon signs, tiny bars stacked on top of each other, music leaking from half-open doors, and conversations spilling onto the streets long after midnight. Tokyo can sometimes feel impossibly reserved. Ni-chome is the opposite. It wears its heart on its sleeve. On a Wednesday night, the neighborhood was already alive. Groups of friends laughed loudly outside bars. Couples disappeared into hidden doorways. Music bounced between buildings while strangers became temporary friends over drinks. The energy was chaotic. Warm. Unapologetically queer. And honestly? Wild. I don't usually end up here. My life in Japan is painfully predictable. Work. Home. Books. Repeat. The kind of routine that sounds boring until you realize I've somehow built a pretty decent life from it. A stable career. Good friends. A bookshelf that's become a structural hazard. A passport full of stamps. On paper, my life was doing just fine. Which made the absence of love feel even stranger. At least, that's what I kept telling myself. Yet somehow, my love life remained under construction. Meanwhile, Kris was somewhere between his third and fourth lemon sour, laughing at something that wasn't even that funny. I ordered one too. My go-to Japanese drink. Cheap. Deceptively easy to drink. The conversations got deeper as the night went on. We talked about work. Family. The people we'd become since our twenties. The strange reality of waking up one day and realizing you're no longer the youngest person in the room. Years had passed since we'd last seen each other in person. Some friendships pick up exactly where they left off. Ours was one of them. Then his phone rang. The screen lit up. Theo. I watched Kris's expression change immediately. Softer somehow. "Answer it," I said. He grinned before picking up. "Hey." The noise of the bar faded into the background while he listened. "Yeah, we're in Shinjuku." A pause. "No, I'm good." Another pause. Then he laughed. "No, I haven't gotten lost." I nearly choked on my drink. A few moments later, he turned the phone toward me. "Theo wants to say hi." Suddenly, I was participating in a relationship I wasn't even a member of. "Hey, Theo." "Hii!!" "Thank you for taking care of Kris." I looked at my friend, who was currently trying to balance three bar snacks in one hand. "Trust me," I said. "Someone has to." Theo laughed. There was a brief silence before he added: "I'm glad he's with you." Simple words. Nothing dramatic. Nothing poetic. Earlier that day, Theo had existed mostly in my imagination. A character built from stories. A possibility. A what-if. But listening to him worry about Kris from thousands of kilometers away, he suddenly felt real. And somehow, that made the version of this trip that never happened hurt a little more. But something about the call stayed with me. Because the entire time, there was something obvious in the way Theo spoke to him. The concern. The consistency. The checking in. Not because he had to. Because he wanted to. And watching it unfold from across the table stirred something unexpected inside me. Jealousy. Not of Theo. Not even of Kris. Just of the feeling. The quiet certainty that somewhere in the world, someone was wondering if you'd eaten dinner. If you got home safely. If you were okay. The call ended a few minutes later. Kris slipped his phone back into his pocket and immediately resumed talking as if nothing had happened. But I sat there for a moment longer. Thinking. Maybe that's what love looks like when it's real. Not grand gestures. Not airport reunions. Not dramatic confessions beneath cherry blossoms. Maybe it's simply remembering to call. Maybe it's checking in. Maybe it's choosing someone repeatedly, even from thousands of kilometers away. I took another sip of my lemon sour. Outside, Shinjuku continued glowing beneath a sea of neon lights. Inside, surrounded by strangers and half-finished drinks, I found myself wondering something I hadn't allowed myself to ask in years. Maybe I don't miss being in a relationship. Maybe I miss being cared for. And somewhere between the seventh neon sign, the fourth lemon sour, and a love story that technically wasn't mine, I realized: The loneliest part of being alone isn't having nobody beside you. It's having nobody waiting for you.
A story begins with a change of plans.
